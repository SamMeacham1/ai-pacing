How we get these numbers

1. PolicyLimits how fast frontier labs push AI capabilities forward.
2. AI progressEach new capability level reaches customers later.
3. DemandThe part of AI demand that waits on better models grows more slowly.
4. InvestmentHyperscalers and labs buy fewer chips and build fewer data centres.
5. Markets and the economyCompany values fall, and lower investment and stock wealth slow GDP.

Middle estimates. Each result is the median across 2,000 simulated futures, which vary the uncertain inputs (for example, how much AI demand waits on better models and how labs respond to the policy). Half the futures give a larger effect and half a smaller one. Setting every input to its central value instead gives somewhat smaller effects (for a 5% compute limit, about 2.1% off the S&P 500 rather than 2.6%), because the futures with large losses are more extreme than those with small ones.

Full enforcement. We assume no capabilities compute gets past the rules. With some evasion, a pause or a compute limit slows capabilities less.

The economy. We translate lower AI investment and lower stock wealth into GDP and unemployment using ratios from the Federal Reserve's FRB/US model. About three quarters of AI investment is imported (mostly chips), so GDP falls far less than investment does. The Federal Reserve responds as it normally would. We report GDP and unemployment for 2027 and 2028 only, because the FRB/US results we draw on stop at 2028.

Why a pause moves stocks so little. Compute keeps growing during a pause, so capabilities catch up within a few years afterwards. Markets value companies on many years of earnings, and a temporary pause changes few of them. Investment still drops sharply while the pause lasts, which is why GDP dips in 2027 and 2028.
